What the work decides

A lawyer uses judgment to connect an agreement to an outcome. It runs on knowledge, on memory of how past calls went, and on the seven parts laid out below. A machine is starting to learn some of those parts by watching, and how far that goes is the question on this page. Trusting it with any of them takes the same two things it takes to trust a lawyer: a record of the calls, and a person who answers for them.

01
Where judgment comes from

Seven things happen when a person stops to make a call

Something reads wrong before anyone can say why. The next step is a decision about what kind of problem this is and whose, and only then comes the research, the precedent and the draft language. Weighing the tradeoffs comes after that, without anyone handing over the weights: risk against speed, this deal against the precedent it sets. A look ahead follows, to how the other side reads it and how it looks in a dispute three years out, and at some point more analysis stops helping. Then a name goes on the result.

The machine took the research first, because research has an answer you can check. It is learning what to flag, and how the department weighs tradeoffs, from the corrections lawyers make, and those corrections are the memory the last page was about. It can also forecast some of how a term will play out.

The other three stay with a person. Framing the problem takes a relationship with the business, deciding when to stop takes having something at stake, and signing takes being someone a board or a court can hold to it. The machine has none of those.

02
How a machine learns it

The machine learns a call by watching where the lawyer changes its work

Every change a lawyer makes to a draft the machine produced is a call it could not make, and counting those changes at each part gives you a map of where to look. Where the count stays high, keep a person there, or write the rule and teach it. Where it falls over a few months, either that part has gone routine or the lawyer has stopped looking, and the rest of this section is about telling those two apart.

The last page had four ways memory gets into a machine. Three of them can carry a call, and from the side of one senior lawyer on the team they look like this: ask her, show her two, or watch her. Whichever way it comes in, the loop after that is the same: the machine proposes, the lawyer corrects, the correction gets filed under the kind of matter it was, and when the same correction keeps showing up it becomes a candidate rule that someone senior signs into the playbook. Build that loop; most departments have only the first two steps of it.

Ask her

State the criteria, then score against them

By default every position gets written down and every draft gets checked against the list.

What you get

A record anyone can read and contest.

What you miss

Ask her what she checks for and she names a handful, because the rest are reading reflexes she has never had to put into words.

Show her two

Which of these would you send

She sees two drafts and picks one, and across enough pairs her preference comes through.

What you get

Her real ordering, faster than any list captures it.

What you miss

The machine learns what she prefers and never learns why, because the choice carries no reasons with it.

Watch her

Keep what she changed

The corrections are already happening on every draft, and in most departments they get made and kept nowhere a machine can learn from.

What you get

A signal the department has already paid for.

What you miss

A correction carries no reason. Ask for one at the moment or it never exists, and the one she types may be written for the box.

Most dashboards measure the wrong thing. A falling review rate tells you nothing on its own, because a lawyer who trusts the machine and a lawyer who stopped reading produce the same number. Measure the gate instead: how often a change by the lawyer changed the outcome, how often a second reviewer disagrees, and whether the rules that got promoted held up in the deals they touched. Plant a few defective drafts in the queue, because an empty override log means something only if the lawyers caught the plants. Route a slice of matters to full human review no matter how confident the score is, so the signal stays alive. Keep the machine off the job of grading itself: a lawyer writes the criteria, a senior one approves them, and a sample gets checked against human calls.

03
How you trust it

What the record of a call has to show

If a machine took part in a call, the lawyer who signed should be able to show six things about it, in words a lawyer would use. Together they are the trace of the call.

01
What it read
Which agreements, which version of the playbook, which saved preferences, which past matters. The memory in force when it made its proposal, with the date on each piece.
02
What rule it applied
The position, the fallback, the walk-away it worked from, and who wrote that rule and why.
03
What it produced
The draft, the redline, the recommendation, as it left the machine.
04
What the lawyer changed, and why
Every place the lawyer overrode the machine, with the reason given at the time.
05
Who signed
The person who stood behind the result, and what authority they had to do it.
06
What it learned
What got written back to memory from this call, what got rewritten, and whether anyone approved it before it runs on the next deal.

The trace has to sit somewhere the people who run the machine cannot rewrite, because a trail the operator can edit is a story, and it has to show the call was made and who made it. Whether it was a good call is a separate question, and another lawyer answers it, against the outcome.

A company lawyer owes the company loyalty and care, and can be held to both. A machine can follow every instruction it is given and still owe nothing, because it has nothing to lose. So the duty stays with the lawyer even when the machine did most of the work, and the machine cannot vouch for its own part in it. With the record above, the lawyer who signed can show she carried the duty through that work.

Judgment is built out of parts. The machine takes the ones with an answer you can check and learns the ones it can watch, and what it learns belongs to the department once someone signs it. Whatever it cannot take needs a stake and a name. The record shows what the machine knew, what it did, what the lawyer changed, and who answered for the result.
